Ontario Bill 140: What It Means for Your Community and Your Projects

Ontario's Building Ontario Faster Act, 2026 accelerates permitting and approvals across the province, with direct implications for Treaty 9 communities and resource development in Northwestern Ontario.

Introduced October 27, 2026, Bill 140 creates a faster regulatory environment for infrastructure, mining, and resource projects. Communities and project proponents need to understand how these changes affect consultation, rights, and readiness.

What Is Bill 140?

Ontario's Building Ontario Faster Act, 2026 accelerates permitting and approvals across the province. For First Nations in Northwestern Ontario, it directly speeds up the regulatory conditions for Ring of Fire road construction, mining exploration, and resource development on Treaty 9 lands.

Introduced October 27, 2026, Bill 140 amends 21 Acts and enacts two new ones. It builds on Bill 5 (2025), which designated the Ring of Fire as Ontario's first Special Economic Zone. Together, the two bills form a layered framework designed to accelerate critical mineral development in Northwestern Ontario.

Why This Matters Now

Accelerated approvals create urgency for both communities and proponents. Documented land use, clear positions, and well-planned engagement are increasingly important before projects enter the pipeline.

What Changes

A Fast-Track Road Construction Template

The new Building Highway 69 Faster Act exempts a Northern Ontario highway expansion from environmental assessment and allows early works — road clearing, bridge construction, and land remediation — to proceed before the assessment is complete. This establishes a fast-track template directly applicable to Ring of Fire access corridors.

Environmental Participation Tools Are Removed

Bill 140 repeals core Environmental Bill of Rights provisions, removing community rights to request reviews, investigations, and environmental protection actions.

Archaeological Assessment Exemptions

The legislation creates new exemptions from archaeological assessment requirements under the Ontario Heritage Act. This is a significant concern because Ontario's own policy identifies areas within 300 metres of water as having high archaeological potential, covering virtually the entire Ring of Fire footprint.

The Duty to Consult Remains

The constitutional Duty to Consult and Accommodate remains intact, but the removal of participation tools means communities must be more proactive to exercise those rights effectively.

What This Means for Treaty 9

Every proposed Ring of Fire access road, mining claim, and exploration project sits within Treaty 9 territory. The fast-track road template, archaeological exemptions, and Environmental Bill of Rights repeal all apply directly to this region.

First Nations from Attawapiskat, Neskantaga, Marten Falls, and other Treaty 9 communities are seeking intervener status in legal challenges to both bills, citing the right to free, prior, and informed consent under UNDRIP.

Communities that are organized and documented before projects enter the pipeline will be in a stronger negotiating position than those responding under pressure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Bill 140 eliminate the Duty to Consult?

No. The Duty to Consult remains in place. What changes are the procedural tools available to communities to participate in and challenge approvals.

Do Ring of Fire access roads fall under the new fast-track framework?

The Highway 69 model establishes a legislative template for fast-tracked road construction. Whether specific Ring of Fire corridors are designated under similar provisions depends on future regulatory decisions — communities should prepare now.

What is the status of Bill 140?

Bill 140 passed second reading on November 2, 2026, and is progressing through the legislature. Prepare now rather than waiting for royal assent.

Prepare Before the Project Arrives
